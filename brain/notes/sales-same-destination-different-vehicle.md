Whatever level of service someone buys, they arrive at the same destination; the tiers differ only in speed and comfort of the journey, so the pitch should sound the same for every tier.

**The analogy.** Everyone is going to Maui. They can walk or swim (slow, sharks, mosquitoes, might get robbed), take a boat or buses (slower, a bit of Wi-Fi, out of the sun), take normal flights (faster, bad food, security lines) or take a private jet (direct, no layovers, champagne, bags unpacked on arrival). Arrival is assumed, because you shouldn't sell anything that doesn't solve the problem; the variables are speed and quality of the journey, plus how much support they want along the way.

**How to use it.**
1. Sell the destination once ([sales-sell-the-vacation]); don't re-pitch per package.
2. Present tiers as vehicles: how fast, how much support, how comfortable.
3. **Start with the private jet.** Some buyers just want one-on-one, and it anchors price: after a $10,000 one-way flight, $499 sounds easy; after a $4,000 program, a couple of hundred dollars "sounds like nothing". Starting low makes the low price feel big. Hormozi notes you can "squeeze margin" this way.

**When to use.** Any business with several tiers (personal training vs group, done-for-you vs done-with-you). For anchoring mechanics see [models-upsell-anchor]; for spacing tiers see [sales-price-tier-ladder].
