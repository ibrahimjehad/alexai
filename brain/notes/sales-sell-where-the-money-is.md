A tiny share of people hold most of the money and a tiny share of customers produce most of the profit, so build offers and prices for them instead of fighting over the bottom.

**The wealth picture (US household net worth, ~$163T, shrunk to $100).** Bottom 50% hold about $2.50; the next 40% about $28; the next 9% about $38; the top 1% about $32, more than the bottom 90% combined. Top 10% hold ~69%. (Income is also skewed: the top 10% earn ~40%.) One in ten Americans has a $1M+ net worth.

**Nested 80/20.** 20% of customers produce ~80% of profit; within that, ~4% produce ~64%; and the top 1% produce ~51%. Profit concentrates even more than revenue because a big customer rarely costs much more to serve.

**Implications.**
- Most small businesses design for the people they see daily and split the $2.50 a hundred ways. "If you want to make money, go where the money is."
- This only works if your model lets people pay more (premium tiers, [sales-price-tier-ladder]). Expect 99 of 100 to say no to the expensive option; when the whale comes, be ready.
- Absolute beats relative profit: one $10,000 sale costing $2,000 ($8,000 profit) equals 400 sales of a $50 item costing $25.
- Serving the masses profitably needs automation, capital and huge volume (Netflix, Spotify, Tesla), which most service businesses (~78-80% of US businesses) lack. So service businesses should serve the best customers at the highest price.
- Start top-down where you can: a premium first product makes the brand narrative (Tesla Roadster -> cheaper models; "Rolls-Royce Lite" beats "Honda goes luxury") and the operations easier.

**When to use.** Choosing a market, setting prices and tiers, deciding who to target in ads.
