A call-out is whatever grabs the right audience's attention in the first seconds; it is the part of the ad to work on and test most.

## Why it matters
- Each second of an ad sells the next second; the headline is the first sale (Ogilvy: writing the headline spends 80 cents of the ad dollar). Focus effort front to back.
- Hormozi's ads got ~20x more efficient when he put most effort into the first five seconds. The goal: hold them long enough to think "this is for me."
- Based on the "cocktail party effect": amid noise, a single relevant signal (your name) cuts through.
- Call-outs range from hyper-specific (your name — only you look) to totally broad (dropped tray — everyone looks). Aim specific enough to get the right people, broad enough to get as many of them as possible.

## Verbal call-outs (words)
1. **Labels** — words that put people in a group: features, traits, titles, places ("Clark County moms", "gym owners", "remote workers", "I'm looking for..."). People identify strongly with their area, so for local ads the more local the better: local area + type of person is a perennial winner (Americans < Texans < Dallas residents < Irving residents in specificity).
2. **Yes questions** — they answer "yes, that's me" and self-qualify ("Do you wake up to pee more than once a night?", "Do you have a home worth over $400k?").
3. **If-then statements** — if you meet these conditions, then this applies ("If you spend over $100k/month on ads, we can save you 20%+").
4. **Ridiculous results** — bizarre, rare, desirable outcomes ("Massage studio books out two years in advance").

## Non-verbal call-outs (setting and spokesperson)
1. **Contrast** — whatever sticks out in the first seconds: bright colors, sounds, movement, attractive people. Bright shirt beats dull; moving beats still.
2. **Likeness** — visually show the labels people identify with; people buy from people who look, talk and act like them. Broad market → mix ages, ethnicities, genders, personalities; narrow market (e.g. medical devices for seniors) → use people like them. "Quack like a duck": to attract plumbers, dress, talk and film like a plumber. Mascots (Geico Gecko, Tony the Tiger) are enduring spokespeople that never age or ask for raises. Using a spokesperson other than the owner also makes the business less owner-dependent and more sellable; attractive people convert better and are cheap to hire.
3. **The scene** — show the yes-question or if-then visually: someone tossing in bed (sleep problems), a pear beside an hourglass (body shape), a room stacked with junk, a rock hitting a window, a local landmark.

Use verbal and non-verbal call-outs together when the platform allows.

## When to use
Writing the opening of any ad or content piece; the first thing to test when an ad underperforms.
