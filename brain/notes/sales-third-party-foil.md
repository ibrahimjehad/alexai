Instead of challenging the prospect, tell them what you told a past customer who was just like them and raised the same concern, then bought and succeeded.

**Script shape.**
1. React positively, even to a money objection: "That's amazing." (They'll be surprised.)
2. "It reminds me of [Janet]. She was just like you: same age, two kids, said it was a lot of money. Mind if I share what I told her?"
3. Deliver the blunt reframe to Janet: "It's good that it's a lot of money, because it means you'll be committed. You've always known what it takes; you've bought cheaper things before and they didn't work because it didn't hurt enough if they failed."
4. Bring it back as a question: "If you actually do this, do you have any doubt you'll hit the goal?" "No." "Then you should be more convinced than anyone."
5. Ask for the sale.

**Why.** You attack an idea held by someone next to them, never the prospect, so rapport survives while the hard truth lands. Two strong foils: someone who bought earlier today, or your biggest success story.

**Ep 33 adds.** The rule in its earliest form: "whenever you want to talk to someone about their circumstances, you never talk to them"; you talk about someone just like them (same job, same situation) who came in yesterday and reached the same conclusion, then ask "Mind if I share the story I told her?" That episode's story is the identity-and-priorities close ([sales-vote-with-your-dollars]). Ep 22 uses the same move for limiting beliefs, with several named customers per excuse ([sales-limiting-belief-customer-stories]).

**When to use.** Within AAA's associate step ([sales-aaa-framework]), especially for money and avoidance objections.
