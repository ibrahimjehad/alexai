Lawyers don't ask questions they don't know the answer to and most people don't propose without knowing the answer, so don't pitch or ask for the sale until you're confident they'll say yes.

**Rules.**
1. **Hit every milestone first.** If the prospect hasn't clarified why they need a solution, you don't ask for the sale. "I just want more info" is not a reason: "Cool, but what did you want the information to solve?"
2. **Stay in the question loop** (questions, restatements, clarifying) until you find the unexposed thread that makes them say "Aha, that's the issue" ([sales-three-things-to-say]).
3. **Let them ask for the pitch.** Ideally they say "How do I work with you?"; at minimum you ask "Would you like to know how the program works? I think it might be a good fit," so they give permission.
4. **Refuse an early pitch.** If they demand "just tell me how it works" too soon: "I don't think we're ready for that. I need to understand more about your business or it won't make sense." This retakes the frame; if they're ordering you around, you won't close.
5. **Out of time? Book another call.** Don't jump the gun with five minutes left when you know the answer is no.
6. **Read the first five to ten minutes.** Experienced reps can tell by then whether this will close; if not, shift the frame with harder questions ([sales-closers-ask-hard-questions]).

**Why.** Before you get to the pitch, the person must already be sold on working with you; the pitch only confirms it. Wasting efficiency early (bragging about your company) is what eats the time you need here.

**Tension with asking often.** Later episodes stress that top closers ask the most times ([sales-ask-the-most-times]). Both hold: earn the first ask through discovery, then ask repeatedly once you have.

**When to use.** Every consultative sale; coaching reps who pitch too soon.
