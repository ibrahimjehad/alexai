Choose the frame (the reality) you want the prospect to live in, then make every detail of the call and environment consistent with it; one mismatched edge pops the frame.

**The idea.** Prospects only know what you show them: your words, pauses, side remarks, how much you need the sale, the room. The best closers don't overcome objections better, they set rules of engagement so objections rarely come up. Hormozi's metaphor is the Trainman from *The Matrix*: in his own program he decides who gets on the train. "I am the train man. These are the rules."

**Steps.**
1. Pick the frame that would make the sale obvious (premium, exclusive, oversubscribed, a bargain).
2. Ask: what else would have to be true if this frame were real? How would the rep sound, sit, pause and talk about capacity? What would the room, phone answering, paperwork look like?
3. Make every "edge of the painting" match, and cut anything that doesn't. Say nothing untrue; let their imagination fill gaps.
4. Fit the frame to the reality you have and give the reason behind odd details ([sales-example-apartment-gym-frame]).

**Congruence example.** A high-end establishment he read about used a British-accented phone answerer and gold-and-black envelopes so every touchpoint matched "premium". Many businesses claim premium while half their details say otherwise.

**Benchmarks.** He puts a typical, appropriately priced B2B phone close rate at about 30-35%, aims for 85%+, and reports 83% hard close on walk-ins for his in-person fitness programs and 90%+ on supplements, because it didn't feel like a sale.

**Levels.** CLOSER is sales 101, AAA is 201, this is 301. A sales director who taught it to his team saw a meaningful lift.

**When to use.** After the script is solid, to move close rates from benchmark to exceptional, and for any premium or in-person sale.
