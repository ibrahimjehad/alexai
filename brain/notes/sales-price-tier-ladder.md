Price each new tier at 5 to 10 times the one below, expect about 20% (1 in 5 to 1 in 10) to take it, and only add a tier if it at least doubles revenue.

**Why the jumps are big.** Spending power differs by orders of magnitude between customer groups. Tiers at $100, $129 and $139 are the same price to the buyer: a lot for most people, trivial for the top 10%. A $1,000 core offer's next rung is $5,000-10,000, not $1,500.

**The maths.** 10 customers: 8 at $10/month ($80) plus 2 at $50/month ($100) doubles revenue. If the base tier already covers overhead (say $70 cost, $10 profit), and the new tier's $100 costs $20 to deliver, profit goes from $10 to $90. Four-tier illustration with 1,000 customers: ~800 at $10/month, ~160 at $100, ~32 at $1,000, ~8 at $10,000 (his on-air numbers were rougher).

**Hormozi's own ladder (Acquisition.com advisory).** Low-priced community plans (Skool hobby and pro tiers), then $5,000 (L1), $35,000 (L2), $135,000 (L3), then portfolio companies. Built over years.

**Rules.**
1. 5-10x the price, and make it something you'd be happy to deliver at that price. If it's "too much work": "we have value and we have price, move one of them" - either do less or charge more (he says charge more; extra delivery is often ~5% of the new price).
2. Expect more nos. "The sweet spot isn't the most yeses, it's the most money." Don't test the big tier on the bottom 50% and conclude it fails.
3. The top tier also anchors: even if few buy, the rest look like a good deal.
4. Add tiers one at a time; start as high up the ladder as you can (easier to serve one $100k client than 1,000 $100 ones; wealthy clients are often less demanding because the price is a smaller share of their net worth).

**When to use.** Designing upsells, packages and continuity tiers; diagnosing why upsells add little revenue.
